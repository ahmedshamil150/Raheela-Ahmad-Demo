Communication in Relationships: What Helps and What Hurts

By Raheelaa Ahmad · Published 22 October 2026

Communication in a relationship is not about talking more — it is about being understood without causing harm, and feeling safe enough to be honest. Most couples who struggle are not short of things to say; they are caught in patterns — criticism, defensiveness, contempt, silence — that turn ordinary disagreements into distance. Those patterns can be changed, with support in person in Lahore or online.

In short: couples rarely fight about the dishes. They fight about feeling unheard, unimportant or unsafe to speak. Learn the four patterns that damage relationships, what healthy exchanges actually sound like, and how to change your half of the dance — which is enough to change the whole thing.

What is communication in a relationship?

Every exchange does two jobs at once: it carries information (“the car needs fuel”) and it carries relationship message (“am I important to you?”). Couples get into trouble when the second message is sent by accident — a rushed reply lands as dismissal, a practical question lands as an attack.

Healthy communication therefore has two halves: saying things clearly and kindly, and hearing your partner without mounting a defence while they are still speaking. Neither half works without emotional safety — the confidence that honesty will not be used as a weapon later.

The patterns that hurt

Marriage researcher John Gottman identified four communication styles so damaging they are called the four horsemen of relationship breakdown:

  • Criticism: attacking character instead of the behaviour — “you never think about anyone but yourself” rather than “I felt overlooked when my mother called”
  • Contempt: eye-rolling, mockery, sarcasm, mimicking — the single strongest predictor of a relationship ending, because it communicates disgust
  • Defensiveness: deflecting every point with excuses or a counter-accusation, which tells your partner their concern will never land
  • Stonewalling: shutting down, walking off, going silent — often after feeling flooded, but experienced by the other as abandonment of the conversation

Add score-keeping (“I did this last time”), mind-reading (“you should have known”), dragging up old injuries and phones at dinner, and most relationship arguments are fully explained.

Why the same argument keeps returning

Repeating fights feel like evidence that nothing changes; they are usually evidence of an unmet need. The bins, the phone, the in-laws and the money are the surface. Underneath sit older questions: do I matter? Am I respected? Can I rely on you?

Couples also get locked into a pursue-withdraw cycle: one partner escalates — louder, sharper, more questions — while the other retreats into silence or leaving the room. Each behaviour confirms the other’s fear (they do not care / they are attacking), so both keep doing exactly what makes it worse. The cycle, not either person, is the enemy.

What healthy communication sounds like

  • Soften the start. How a conversation opens predicts how it ends — begin gently, even when angry
  • Use “I” statements: “I felt hurt when plans changed without a word” — about the moment, not their character
  • Ask for what you want, directly. Hints and traps set your partner up to fail; a clear request can actually be answered
  • Listen to understand, not to rebut. When it is their turn, your only job is to reflect back what you heard
  • Validate even in disagreement: “I see why you would feel that way” does not mean conceding — it means they are not crazy
  • Repair early. A touch, a joke, an apology, “can we start again?” — successful couples repair within minutes, not hours
  • Choose timing. Exhausted, hungry or mid-front-of-children is not the moment for heavy topics

Listening is the harder half

Most people listen while preparing their reply. Real listening is slower and rarer: let them finish without interrupting, reflect the essence back (“so what you’re saying is you felt alone in that decision”), and ask before advising. In Pakistan’s close family structures, conversations often carry more than two people — in-laws, parents, households. Protecting a private, unhurried channel between partners is part of protecting the relationship itself.

How counselling helps

Counselling gives you a neutral room where the pattern can be slowed down until both of you can see it. A therapist names the cycle without taking sides, helps each partner hear the need beneath the other’s tone, and lets you practise a different response while it is safe. Skills are taught directly — softened startups, “I” statements, repair attempts — but the deeper work is emotional: understanding what each of you fears and how that fear drives your habits.

Our couples counselling page covers how sessions work, and the compatibility questionnaire is a useful starting point for couples who want to understand their patterns before therapy. If your partner is not ready to come, individual work on your half of the pattern still shifts the system — through individual therapy.

What happens in a session

The first session gives both of you space to say what is happening — not to win the case, but to be understood. Together you identify the recurring cycle, what triggers it and what it costs. From there the plan is practical: where to interrupt the pattern, which skills to build first and what to practise between sessions. Sessions usually last around 50 to 60 minutes.

Counselling in person in Lahore or online

Choose an in-person session in Lahore if you would prefer a calm, private room for two. Choose an online session if travel or schedules make meeting difficult — working from home together is common and works well. Both formats are available, and you can start with either.

When to get help quickly

Reach out sooner rather than later if arguments have turned to contempt or silence that lasts days, if you are staying together only for appearances, or if fear — rather than frustration — is becoming part of the relationship. Where there is intimidation or abuse, individual safety comes first: contact a trusted person or local support rather than couples work. Otherwise, counselling early — while goodwill remains — is far easier than after years of erosion.

Frequently asked questions

What is the biggest predictor of a relationship breaking down?

Contempt — eye-rolling, mockery, sarcasm and a sense of superiority toward your partner. Research by the Gottman Institute found contempt to be the strongest signal of breakdown, precisely because it communicates disgust rather than disagreement.

Is it normal for couples to argue about the same things?

Yes. Repeating arguments usually mean the same underlying need is not being met — for reassurance, respect, help or closeness. The topic is the surface; the pattern underneath is what needs a different ending.

Can communication really be learned?

It can. Communication is a set of habits, and habits change with awareness and practice — softening how you start a complaint, listening before defending, and repairing after ruptures. Couples learn these skills at any stage or age.

What if my partner will not come to counselling?

Change usually still works. A relationship is a system: when one person stops pursuing with criticism or withdrawing in silence, the dance shifts and the other person responds differently. Individual sessions are often the starting point.

How long does couples counselling take?

There is no fixed number. Some couples come for a handful of sessions around one issue; others work over several months on long-standing patterns. After an initial assessment you both get a clear idea of the likely focus and length.
