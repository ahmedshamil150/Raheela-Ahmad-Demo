Life Coaching vs Therapy: What Is the Difference and How to Choose

By Raheelaa Ahmad · Published 9 November 2026

Life coaching and therapy both involve a supportive professional asking good questions — easy to mistake for the same thing. They are not. Life coaching is future-focused and goal-driven: clarifying what you want and building a plan to reach it. Therapy is practised by licensed mental health professionals who can assess, diagnose and treat conditions like anxiety, depression and trauma, working with the past as well as the present.

In short: coaching helps you move forward from a stable baseline; therapy helps you heal. Many people use both.

What is the difference between life coaching and therapy?

Three questions draw the line clearly:

  • What is the focus? Coaching centres on goals — a career decision, a move abroad, confidence, time management. Therapy centres on mental and emotional health — understanding symptoms, processing experiences and recovering steadiness
  • Who is qualified to do it? Therapy is a regulated health profession: licensure, graduate training and supervised clinical hours are required. Coaching has no licence; credentials such as the International Coaching Federation’s ACC, PCC and MCC are voluntary marks of quality you should look for
  • What is the time orientation? Coaching works mainly in the present and future — where you are, where you want to be, what blocks you. Therapy works across past, present and future, because earlier experiences often shape current patterns

What is life coaching?

The International Coaching Federation defines coaching as “partnering with clients in a thought-provoking and creative process that inspires them to maximize their personal and professional potential” — on the premise that the client, not the coach, is the expert on their own life.

Practically, a coaching session is a calm, confidential, focused space where something overwhelming gets broken into manageable steps. Typical territory: career decisions, planning a move abroad, choosing subjects or a university, rebuilding confidence, or leading under pressure. In Raheelaa Ahmad’s coaching, the work looks across five core areas of life — health, relationships, career, finances and spirituality — using the FTBA framework (feelings, thoughts, beliefs and actions) to realign what you feel and believe with the life you want to create, alongside practices for clearing older energetic patterns. The aim is lasting clarity and confidence, not just one solved problem.

What is therapy?

Psychotherapy — counselling or talk therapy — is practised by licensed professionals (psychologists, clinical social workers, counsellors) who completed graduate training, supervised clinical hours and licensing requirements, bound by professional codes and confidentiality duties. It can assess and treat clinical conditions: anxiety disorders, depression, trauma, grief and recurring emotional or relational patterns.

How the two differ in practice

  • Focus: coaching — goals, decisions, performance, accountability · therapy — symptoms, emotional healing, understanding patterns
  • Training: coaching — voluntary certifications, no standardised requirement · therapy — graduate education, supervised hours, licensing exams
  • Regulation: coaching — no central governing body (the ICF is the main accrediting body, membership optional) · therapy — licensed and overseen by professional boards
  • Scope: coaches are legally not permitted to diagnose or treat mental health conditions · therapists are trained and authorised to do exactly that
  • Privacy and cost: therapy clients hold legal confidentiality rights and sessions are covered by insurance or public systems in many places · coaching clients have no equivalent legal privacy guarantee and usually pay out of pocket

Can you do coaching and therapy at the same time?

Yes, and it is more common than people assume. Cleveland Clinic notes that many people work with a therapist and a life coach simultaneously — therapy for processing the past and healing in the present, coaching for the forward push of goals and accountability. The combination suits demanding seasons — recovering while planning a change, or steadying yourself after burnout.

If you do both, keep each professional informed so the work stays coordinated. Ethical practice includes the boundary between them: a coach refers you to a therapist when clinical issues surface, and a therapist may suggest a coach once you are stable and want structure. If anxiety is what weighs you down, start with our guide to anxiety therapy; if work pressure dominates, see burnout and workplace stress.

How do I choose? A simple test

Ask two honest questions: Am I essentially stable, or am I in distress? and Is my need a goal or a symptom?

  • Lean toward coaching if: your mental health is steady; you face a decision, transition or ambition; you want structure, clarity and accountability between sessions
  • Lean toward therapy if: distress persists most days; anxiety, low mood, numbness or old wounds keep interfering; relationships repeat the same painful pattern; you need a confidential space with a clinician trained to assess what is happening
  • Consider both if: you are in a demanding chapter — a big move, leadership stress, recovery after a loss — and want healing and forward motion at once

One statistic worth knowing: research cited by NPR found that 25% to 50% of people working with a life coach have mental health conditions that require a higher level of support — which is why honest self-assessment matters. If unsure, book a consultation with each type of professional and compare how the conversations feel.

Red flags to watch for

  • Cure claims. No one should promise to heal you completely or claim that one method is the only way
  • Diagnosis from an unqualified helper. Coaches cannot diagnose mental health conditions; if someone labels your condition without clinical training, walk away
  • No written contract. Scope, fees and limitations should be set out before you begin
  • Defensiveness to questions. Ask about training and references — a credible professional welcomes it
  • Isolation tactics. Anyone steering you away from family, friends or other professionals is a serious warning sign

When to choose help quickly

One boundary matters: if you are in crisis — persistent thoughts of self-harm, an unsafe home situation, inability to function — that calls for clinical support and, where needed, emergency services. Coaching is not crisis care. Starting with therapy then is right timing, not failure — coaching can come later.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between life coaching and therapy?

Coaching is future-focused and goal-driven — clarifying what you want and planning how to reach it, usually from a stable baseline. Therapy is provided by licensed mental health professionals who can assess, diagnose and treat conditions such as anxiety, depression and trauma, working with the past as well as the present. Coaching is never a substitute for treating a mental health condition.

Can I do life coaching and therapy at the same time?

Yes — it is common and often helpful. Cleveland Clinic notes many people work with a therapist and a life coach simultaneously, especially during demanding seasons: therapy for healing and emotional processing, coaching for momentum and accountability. Keep both professionals aware of each other so the work stays coordinated.

Is life coaching regulated?

There is no central governing body that licences coaches. The International Coaching Federation (ICF) is the field’s main accrediting body, with credentialed levels (ACC, PCC, MCC), but membership is voluntary. So vet carefully: ask about training, references and a written contract covering fees and scope.

Does insurance cover life coaching?

Generally no — life coaches are not healthcare providers, so health plans usually do not cover their fees. Therapy is a regulated health service and is covered by insurance or public systems in many places, depending on where you live. Ask a prospective coach about fees before you begin.

How do I choose between a life coach and a therapist?

Choose therapy if you are dealing with persistent distress, anxiety, low mood, trauma effects, or patterns you cannot shift alone — and especially if you are in crisis. Choose coaching if you feel basically stable and need clarity, decisions, structure and accountability toward a goal. If you sit between the two, one consultation with each is a reasonable way to decide.
